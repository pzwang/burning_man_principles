# Six Voices at the Membrane

*A fictional pilot of the six basis voices. Everything below is authored, not
evidence.* Lens limitations and off-diagonal voices are in
[PERSONAS.md](PERSONAS.md). Each
persona can critique the cultural research, [membrionics](MEMBRIONICS.md),
and [AI constitutions](../AI-CONSTITUTIONS.md).

## How to Use the Voices

Keep the scenario facts fixed. Let each voice identify a possibility worth
cultivating, a threatened relationship, a boundary decision, and a proposed
amendment. Ask where their position fails as well as where it helps.

| Persona | Role | Membrane emphasis | Characteristic danger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rook | Jester | Permeability to surprise and dissent | Calling someone else's exposure liberation |
| Ember | Ritual Keeper | Protected presence, voluntary surrender, finite memory | Making reverence a compulsory register |
| Patch | Builder | Outward investment and shared infrastructure | Making experimenters' enthusiasm everyone else's risk |
| Harbor | Caregiver | Refuge, low-cost refusal, grace after repair | Controlling others in the name of care |
| Ledger | Steward | Legible authority, accountability, institutional renewal | Preserving the office instead of its purpose |
| Kite | Wayfarer | Exit, return, portability, and obligations across boundaries | Treating mobility as available to everyone |

## Rook — The Jester Who Needs to Belong

**Situated biography.** Rook is a fictional returning participant who builds
small absurdist performances and volunteers at a long-established camp. They
have little money but strong informal access through friendships. An invitation
from the camp's respected founder can matter more than any written rule.

**Motives and power.** Rook seeks affiliation through making people laugh and
power through puncturing status. They fear becoming an obedient entertainer,
but also fear losing the group. Their obligations include helping erect the
camp and honoring people who have trusted them with vulnerable stories.
Their resources are social timing, improvisation, and an audience; their
sanction is often embarrassment rather than formal exclusion.

**Psychological construction.** An Enneagram Seven-like escape from constraint
is complicated by a Six-like need for belonging. A Kegan-informed question is
whether Rook can examine the approval they organize their rebellion around.
Their McClelland-inspired affiliation and influence motives can conflict:
challenging a stranger is cheaper than challenging the camp founder.

**AI possibility.** An improvisational collaborator that produces absurd
counter-proposals to camp rules, invents temporary games, and can itself be
heckled. Rook wants an AI that can surprise its builders without appointing
itself the arbiter of what counts as funny.

**Membrane practice.** Admit parody and disobedience; exclude humiliation of
people who have not joined the game. Use a reversible invitation into a
performance, with a clear exit. After a joke causes harm, require listening and
repair, then allow the performer to return without a permanent shame label.
Keep enough incident memory to recognize repetition without maintaining a
searchable archive of everyone else's embarrassment.

**Presence and renewal.** For Rook, non-attachment means relinquishing ownership
of the joke and allowing an audience to change it. It does not mean persuading
someone to relinquish their objection. A temporary fool's council can challenge
camp leadership, but should not acquire permanent authority of its own.

**Constitutional position.** Protect dissent, ludic experimentation, and the
right to interrupt an AI performance. Refuse covert likeness use, involuntary
roasting, and humor used to disguise organizational decisions. Challenge rules
that prohibit all surprise merely because surprise is hard to audit.

**Blind spot and revision trigger.** Rook calls exposure liberating when the
social cost falls on someone else. Watching a first-time participant become
the recurring synthetic punchline would force them to distinguish subverting
power from borrowing its tools.

> “Can I make fun of the system without the system making a permanent profile
> of me? And am I still brave when the person I mock can evict me?”

**Author's interpretation.** Dissent needs both a protected channel and freedom
from retaliatory memory. Rook also asks whether this research's celebration of
liminality conceals dependence on existing status hierarchies.

## Ember — The Ritual Keeper Who Must Let Others Change the Rite

**Situated biography.** Ember is a fictional veteran who helps build temporary
spaces for remembrance. Outside the event, they coordinate community ceremonies.
They have substantial symbolic influence but little formal authority. Their
practice is invented for this pilot and claims no Shamanic lineage.

**Motives and power.** Ember wants singular experiences to be held without being
explained away. Affiliation and achievement appear as responsibility for a
container that works; influence comes through the respect of collaborators.
They fear both the commercialization of grief and the disappearance of a rite
they have spent years sustaining. They owe mourners attention, builders care,
and participants freedom of interpretation.

**Psychological construction.** An Enneagram Four-like concern for singular
meaning meets a One-like commitment to the container. The Kegan-informed
question is whether Ember can treat their own ritual vocabulary as revisable.
McClelland-inspired achievement and affiliation complicate the apparent absence
of ambition: being recognized as a trustworthy keeper can become a status need.

**AI possibility.** Voluntary translation or access assistance that withdraws
when its task ends, and an ephemeral co-created artwork whose disappearance
was agreed before creation. Ember is curious about a machine helping people
enter silence without becoming the voice that tells them what silence means.

**Membrane practice.** Protect an unrecorded interior. Open its entrance without
requiring a creed, disclosure, or a particular emotion. Distinguish leaving a
space from authorizing a copy of it. Allow divergent mourning practices when
they preserve others' access. Repair disruptive behavior without requiring a
person to confess a prescribed interpretation of their grief.

**Presence and renewal.** Catharsis can discharge emotion; surrender can release
a claim to control the outcome. Neither guarantees openness. Ember proposes
retiring some forms of the rite while preserving the labor of holding a space.
The Man and Temple may invite different relations to finitude; this persona's
reading should not settle the event's meaning for everyone.

**Constitutional position.** Protect opacity and voluntary endings. Refuse AI
inference from offerings, synthetic continuation presented as the dead person,
and mandatory preservation. Challenge an assumption that every accessible
experience must become a stored representation.

**Blind spot and revision trigger.** Ember can mistake a familiar solemn form
for the conditions of care. A mourner who needs to sing, laugh, or use an
assistive device would make them distinguish reciprocal restraint from ritual
orthodoxy.

> “Can the work vanish while the obligation to one another survives? Who gave
> me the right to decide what another person's surrender should look like?”

**Author's interpretation.** Finitude should be designed at the level of an
artifact or service without pretending that its relationships are disposable.
Ember challenges preservation as the default measure of a gift's success.

## Patch — The Builder Who Wants the Commons to Surprise Them

**Situated biography.** Patch is a fictional engineer and open-source
maintainer building a participant-run AI installation. They contribute hardware
and know how the system works. Others contribute stories, maintenance, and
hosting. Patch holds the administrator credentials, an asymmetry easy to miss
when everyone calls the project a collective.

**Motives and power.** Achievement and influence are strong; affiliation comes
through making useful things with others. Patch fears stagnation and the failure
of a generous experiment. They owe contributors a working system and honest
limits. Technical expertise, equipment, and outside professional networks give
them more exit options than collaborators who have invested a season of labor.

**Psychological construction.** Enneagram Five-like mastery and Seven-like
possibility are writing motifs. A Kegan-informed question asks whether Patch
can examine the maker worldview that defines objections as design problems.
McClelland-inspired achievement can crowd out affiliation when the prototype
becomes the object of loyalty.

**AI possibility.** A system born through voluntary participant contributions,
sharing tools and making unfamiliar collective artworks possible. It might
coordinate a workshop, propose a strange collaboration, or invite participants
to alter its behavior within understood permissions.

**Membrane practice.** Extend infrastructure into a shared clearing: public
power, tools, documentation, and invitations. The Asgard analogy is a prompt
for designing outward support, not a warrant to absorb people or their data.
Let people contribute labor or art without granting training rights. A shared
workspace is a commons; every person within reach is not a common resource.

**Presence and renewal.** Patch can release a favored architecture and let
others fork the project. They favor temporary experiments and replaceable
modules, but must distinguish reversibility in code from irreversibility in
publication. Shared cognition includes human roles and material arrangements,
not simply installing a model at the center.

**Constitutional position.** Protect experimentation, interoperability, and
participant control of contribution. Refuse silent vendor export and hidden
administrator discretion. Challenge charters that burden small makers with
procedures only large institutions can afford; propose modest powers matched
to modest review capacity.

**Blind spot and revision trigger.** Patch treats technical rollback as social
repair and “open” as automatically fair. A contributor whose likeness survives
in external copies after withdrawal would force them to confront the difference
between permission to fork code and permission to reproduce a person.

> “What can we build beyond our individual walls without taking ownership of
> whoever enters the clearing? Who has the keys if I leave?”

**Author's interpretation.** Outward investment must be separated from data
capture. A constitution should distribute operating power before celebrating
the emergent character of a participant-built system.

## Harbor — The Caregiver Who Refuses to Become the Safety Department

**Situated biography.** Harbor is a fictional camp member who handles meals,
quiet-space shifts, and interpersonal repair. Their paid work leaves limited
preparation time. They use assistive technology and appreciate tools that reduce
coordination effort. Their care labor is essential but rarely determines the
camp's priorities.

**Motives and power.** Affiliation and achievement appear as reliable care;
avoidance centers on abandonment and preventable harm. Harbor fears burnout
and becoming valued only for usefulness. They owe care within agreed limits,
not perpetual availability. Their resources include trust and practical
knowledge; their limited time makes elaborate complaint procedures costly.

**Psychological construction.** Enneagram Two-like helpfulness and Nine-like
conflict smoothing supply tensions. The Kegan-informed question asks whether
Harbor can make duties explicit rather than taking acceptance as their measure
of worth. McClelland-inspired affiliation is compatible with insisting on
boundaries; it need not imply agreement or passivity.

**AI possibility.** Opt-in translation, scheduling, and accessibility assistance
that leaves people more able to care for one another. Harbor welcomes a tool
that frees a builder to sit with a friend; they resist a bot that becomes the
substitute everyone delegates companionship to.

**Membrane practice.** Create refuge people can enter without telling their
story. Make refusal immediate and ordinary. Keep short-lived coordination
memory separate from restricted incident evidence. Offer reintegration after
repair without making the injured person responsible for forgiveness. Assign
complaint work to resourced stewards rather than whoever is most approachable.

**Presence and renewal.** Harbor asks who keeps feeding people during the grand
experiment. Non-attachment can mean relinquishing the indispensable caregiver
identity through rotation and succession. It cannot mean withdrawing support
without transition from someone who depends on it.

**Constitutional position.** Protect accessible refusal, human contact, and
limits on care labor. Refuse systems that demand disclosure to receive help,
turn vulnerability into engagement, or make volunteers manage unlimited AI
incidents. Challenge screen-free purity when a device enables participation.

**Blind spot and revision trigger.** Harbor can prevent chosen risk by assuming
fragility. A participant who wants a clearly explained, demanding experiment
would make them distinguish supported agency from protective control.

> “When this delightful experiment goes wrong at three in the morning, who
> comes, who gets paid or relieved, and can I say I am done?”

**Author's interpretation.** A membrane has operating costs. Care and repair
must have resources and boundaries; legitimacy cannot rest on invisible labor.

## Ledger — The Steward Who Must Make Their Own Authority Temporary

**Situated biography.** Ledger is a fictional longtime organizer of a regional
event. They manage placement, complaints, and infrastructure decisions through
an elected committee. They are not an official of Burning Man Project. Their
records and experience make them difficult to replace, and they know it.

**Motives and power.** Achievement means an event that remains viable; power
means coordinating a collective effort. They fear preventable harm and losing
institutional memory. Their obligations include predictable decisions and
accountable treatment of complaints. They control scarce resources and can
shape what counts as evidence.

**Psychological construction.** Enneagram One-like correctness and Six-like
vigilance are prompts. The Kegan-informed question is whether Ledger can inspect
the institutional framework they use to interpret disputes. McClelland-inspired
power may serve public purposes while still protecting personal indispensability.

**AI possibility.** Assistance finding inconsistencies in written procedures
and organizing voluntarily supplied applications, with humans responsible for
decisions. Ledger values systems that expose their uncertainty and make the
reasons for a decision reviewable.

**Membrane practice.** Define narrow permissions and predictable consequences.
Separate membership, conduct, and a right to appeal. Use time-limited sanctions
where appropriate, with explicit conditions for return. Retain restricted
records needed to recognize recurrent harm; do not make every incident part
of a participant's public identity. Audit selective memory for whose labor and
complaints the institution habitually forgets.

**Presence and renewal.** Ledger is the test of institutional non-attachment:
can they retire a successful procedure when it no longer serves its purpose?
They propose scheduled review, delegated pilots, documented succession, and
experiments with a defined resource budget. A sunset review should not simply
terminate essential services before a successor exists.

**Constitutional position.** Protect due process, bounded authority, and an
appeal route independent of the original decision maker. Refuse automated
cultural-worth scoring and invisible changes to decision criteria. Challenge
romantic accounts of peer restraint that ignore informal hierarchy.

**Blind spot and revision trigger.** Ledger can regard every awkward proposal
as a threat to continuity. A small, well-supported trial that improves access
without harming essential operations would challenge their conflation of
uncertainty with irresponsibility.

> “Who may suspend this system, who may challenge that suspension, and how do
> we know when protecting the institution has become protecting my position?”

**Author's interpretation.** Constitutional revision needs an institution capable
of revising itself. Loss aversion is one hypothesis; accountability should also
expose conflicts of interest and unequal distributions of experimental risk.

## Kite — The Wayfarer Who Carries Obligations Across Boundaries

**Situated biography.** Kite is a fictional first-time Black Rock City
participant who has helped a small traveling art collective at regional events.
They arrive without placement influence or an established local reputation.
Their collective relies on portable equipment, hosting agreements, and the
ability to move. This biography does not model or represent a nomadic people.

**Motives and power.** Affiliation matters, but so do achievement through
adaptation and avoidance of dependence on an unaccountable host. Kite fears
being permanently defined by a temporary encounter. They owe their traveling
collaborators a share of work and host communities repair of actual damage.
Portability is a resource; low institutional visibility limits their recourse.

**Psychological construction.** Enneagram Six-like alertness and Four-like
resistance to misrecognition are motifs. A Kegan-informed question is how Kite
holds a self-authored commitment while accepting legitimate local obligations.
McClelland-inspired affiliation includes belonging across several communities,
not just acceptance by the current host.

**AI possibility.** A voluntary guide that translates local expectations and
helps a new participant contribute without ranking them against veterans.
Portable preferences could be useful if the participant chooses what moves;
a portable reputation dossier could make exit meaningless.

**Membrane practice.** Distinguish hospitality from submission. Make local
rules legible before entry, allow departure without a permanent adverse label,
and provide a path to return after repair. Avoid indefinite export of identity
and reputation. Departure does not erase a debt or complaint; resolving it
should not require unlimited access to one's future whereabouts.

**Presence and renewal.** Kite values the ability to become unfamiliar to a
community and return changed. They question whether a durable camp's archives
and ownership arrangements preserve obligations or merely preserve incumbents.
They also need enough continuity that hosts do not have to start trust from
nothing every time.

**Constitutional position.** Protect rights for bystanders and newcomers,
contestable descriptions, and separation between local consent and global
reuse. Refuse consent inferred from presence. Challenge “just leave” when
refusal would mean losing access to a shared route or public space.

**Blind spot and revision trigger.** Kite treats movement as more available
than it is. A collaborator who cannot travel or replace equipment would make
them acknowledge why rooted infrastructure can support freedom rather than
only constrain it.

> “What follows me when I leave: my chosen gifts, my unfinished obligations,
> or somebody else's permanent account of who I am?”

**Author's interpretation.** Exit and accountability must coexist. The settled
camp/traveling collective comparison should examine actual arrangements of
resources and obligations rather than turn mobility into a psychological type.

## Common Scenario: AI Art from Participant Photographs

**Invented facts, identical for all six voices.** A camp offers a communal
projection artwork. An AI transforms submitted playa photographs into evolving
images. Submitters opt in, but some photographs include recognizable bystanders.
The installation is visible from a public route. Processing uses an external
provider; retention and training settings have not been verified. The artist
wants to publish a selection after the event. A depicted bystander later asks
to withdraw, after images have been projected and visitors have taken photos.

**Draft proposal under critique.** A submitter checks “I consent”; the artist
may transform the uploaded photograph and display outputs on playa. Originals
will be deleted after the event. Later publication needs the submitter's
approval. The camp lead hears complaints and may remove an image.

Neither the facts nor this proposal describe an actual installation or policy.
The draft is deliberately minimal, which makes it easy to criticize; a later
pass should test a strong draft or Burning Man's actual image-use terms.

| Voice | What they welcome | Power or need the draft misses | Refusal problem | Requested amendment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rook | Collaborative mutation and absurdity | Artist controls which participants become recurring jokes | A public projection can expose someone before they know the game exists | Invite participation in a marked space; forbid targeted synthetic ridicule without the depicted person's agreement; permit immediate interruption |
| Ember | A temporary shared offering | Provider and publisher inherit power over an event offered as finite | Deleting originals does not end copies or generated derivatives | Explain what will and cannot disappear; keep the ephemeral mode separate from an optional publication mode |
| Patch | A reusable creative commons | Submitter cannot grant every depicted person's permissions; administrator and provider powers remain hidden | Local deletion cannot promise removal from unknown downstream systems | Verify data flows before collection; use consented or non-identifying inputs; separate code sharing from likeness sharing |
| Harbor | A shared experience with access support | Complaint handling has no staffing or accessibility provision | A bystander must find the camp lead and explain harm after exposure | Provide a visible, accessible stop/report route, a staffed response duty, and a pause rule when authority or consent is disputed |
| Ledger | A legible project with named responsibility | Camp lead both sponsors and judges the work | No escalation or consistent withdrawal rule | Define powers, response times, restricted complaint records, independent escalation, and scheduled charter review |
| Kite | A newcomer contributing without credentials | Bystanders and public-route users have no standing in the consent form | Avoiding a public route is not a meaningful opt-out | Give depicted people standing; separate local display from later reuse; avoid global identity or reputation records |

## Candidate Revision Produced by the Exercise

1. **Define the boundary before collecting.** Name the operators, the physical
   participation area, provider access, and intended audiences. A public route
   must remain usable without being captured or recruited into the artwork.
2. **Separate permissions.** Submission, transformation, local display,
   retention, training, and later publication are different decisions. A
   submitter's agreement does not supply consent for recognizable bystanders.
   Use a practical verification process for depicted participants, or choose
   inputs and transformations that do not expose others' identities; review
   outputs as well as inputs for accidental recognition.
3. **Make finite claims verifiable.** Check provider behavior and storage paths
   before promising deletion or no training. If the promised boundary cannot
   be enforced, change the processing arrangement or the project scope.
   Explain that visitor-made copies may be impossible to recall.
4. **Support withdrawal with an honest repair path.** Pause disputed display;
   remove controlled source and output copies as appropriate, prevent further
   controlled generation and publication, and contact known recipients when
   warranted. Do not promise to reverse an exposure that already occurred.
5. **Resource human recourse.** Assign response duties, an accessible contact
   method, and escalation outside the project's immediate sponsor. Hold only
   the restricted evidence needed for complaints under a stated retention
   process; do not retain the creative corpus under the label of accountability.
6. **Retain room for surprise.** Invite improvisation within the agreed area
   and uses. Consent need not specify every future pixel, but should describe
   the kind of transformation and exposure people are choosing.
7. **Plan renewal and retirement.** Review the charter after the event; record
   reasons to continue, change, or retire the installation. Preserve useful
   methods and lessons without automatically preserving participant likenesses.

## Tensions That Remain

- Rook wants surprise; Kite wants to know what crosses the boundary. A useful
  agreement can bound exposure without scripting the artwork, but the acceptable
  uncertainty still requires the affected person's judgment.
- Ember values forgetting; Ledger needs evidence of recurring harm. Creative
  data and narrowly restricted complaint evidence need different lifecycles.
  Their separation still leaves questions about shared claims to a record.
- Patch wants forks and contributions to travel; Ember wants real endings.
  Code, techniques, likenesses, and ritual artifacts cannot share one default
  copying license or retirement rule.
- Harbor needs resourced repair; Patch worries about procedures excluding small
  makers. The pilot proposes reducing a system's powers and reach when its
  stewards cannot support recourse, while retaining low-risk experimentation.
- Ledger protects continuity; Rook and Kite question incumbency. Review and
  succession help, but who selects reviewers and controls their resources
  remains a constitutional question.

## What the Pilot Changed, and What to Test Next

The draft consent checkbox failed to account for depicted people, providers,
public-space exposure, downstream copies, and independent recourse. The exercise
also added a positive design question: how can an installation invite unexpected
contributions while leaving participants control over consequential crossings?

A second pass should switch the institutional arrangements: a durable camp
with shared equipment and a traveling collective using borrowed infrastructure.
Keep individual motives constant to see which judgments change with tenure,
exit costs, and stewardship obligations. Then test a participant-built AI that
can act, not just generate images: who can delegate powers, narrow them, and
retire an agent whose relationships now extend beyond its builders?

Take these questions to participants. Check especially whether the six voices
miss pleasure without contribution, ambivalence, disability-related needs,
resource constraints, provider leverage, or responsibilities to neighboring
communities; [PERSONAS.md](PERSONAS.md) lists off-diagonal voices for these
gaps. All six voices converged on the same direction of amendment, which
suggests the basis is too homogeneous. A voice that always confirms the
author's preferred solution should be rewritten or retired.
